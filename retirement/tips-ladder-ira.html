TIPS Ladder Inside an IRA

Original planning page, not a reprint. Yields are the U.S. Treasury real par curve for November 3, 2026. Costs are the present value of the real spending stream on that curve. Not a broker quote. Not tax, legal, or personalized advice. Check the order ticket and a Social Security estimate before you buy.

The length answer

Build 20 years. Shape it. Do not build a flat 30, and do not stop at 5.

A TIPS ladder is a spending floor, not the portfolio. Fat rungs until Social Security starts. Thin rungs after that, equal to essential spending minus Social Security, in today's dollars. Social Security already has a COLA. Do not inflation-gross it a second time.

  • Shorter than the bridge is too short. Stop work around 60 and claim at 70, and the dangerous window is about 10 years. A 5-year ladder does not cover it. Years 1–3 can be Treasury bills inside the IRA if you want simpler cash. Inflation protection is the point from about year 5 out.
  • 20 years is the default. That is the bridge plus a thinner decade after Social Security, so a bad market in your 60s does not force stock sales for groceries.
  • 25–30 is optional, and only for the thin tail. Treasury only sells TIPS out to 30 years. That is the ceiling. Stretch the post-Social Security gap toward 30 only while long real yields stay near 3%, and only for money you are sure you will spend. Do not lock a house down payment, a truck, or the tax cash for a Roth conversion in a 30-year rung.
  • Do not ladder the whole IRA. Ladder the essential gap. Leave the rest in the guardrail sleeve. The ladder is what lets the guardrails run without selling stocks in a crash. It is not the growth engine.

Why the IRA, not TreasuryDirect

Buy the ladder in the traditional IRA at the broker. TreasuryDirect is the other door: individual or entity accounts, auction bids, $100 minimum. A bank, broker, or dealer is how an IRA buys Treasuries. TreasuryDirect is not an IRA custodian.

Phantom income is the reason the IRA wins. In a taxable account, Treasury says federal tax is due each year on interest, and that a change in principal during the year may affect your taxes. The IRS makes you report the inflation bump to principal as original issue discount even though you did not receive the cash. Inside a traditional IRA that annual tax does not hit. Distributions are taxable in the year you receive them. Maturity inside the IRA is not the taxable event. The withdrawal is.

State tax cuts the other way. Treasury interest is free of state and local income tax when you hold the bond yourself. An IRA withdrawal is generally just IRA income. If you move to a state that taxes retirement distributions, the state exemption does not come through the IRA. Do not park a big ladder in taxable just to chase that exemption unless a tax pro runs the numbers. The federal phantom-income hit usually dominates.

A Roth IRA also works, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Roth space is scarce. Prefer stocks there. Default home for the ladder is the traditional IRA.

RMDs start at 73 under the current IRS FAQ, including traditional IRAs, even if you are already retired. A ladder that is most of the traditional IRA will eventually be forced to pay out more than the maturing rung. Keep other IRA assets so the RMD can come from somewhere else, or do not build a 30-year ladder that the IRS will tear up. Confirm your own required beginning date on IRS.gov. The FAQ states age 73. It does not spell out a later increase.

What you are buying

Treasury sells TIPS for 5, 10, or 30 years. The coupon is fixed at auction and is never less than 0.125%. Interest is paid every six months on the inflation-adjusted principal, so the dollar coupon moves. At maturity you get the higher of adjusted principal or original par. You do not get less than original par.

Sort by real yield, not coupon. A 0.125% coupon can still yield 2.5% real if you buy it below par.

Auctions will not fill a ladder. New bonds land on a set calendar. 5-year: April and October, reopenings June and December. 10-year: January and July, reopenings March, May, September, and November. 30-year: February, reopening August. The in-between years are older bonds on the secondary market. That is where almost every rung comes from.

A TIPS fund (TIP, SCHP, VTIP, and the like) is not a ladder. No maturity date. Those funds lost money in 2022 when real yields rose, which is exactly the year a retiree does not want the "safe" sleeve down. A rung you hold to the year you plan to spend does not have that problem. The statement value can still bounce. Ignore it.

How to build it

  1. Write the gap in today's dollars. Essentials only. Not a house down payment, not conversion taxes, not a one-time truck. Those are cash, not rungs.
  2. Shape the years. Full gap before Social Security. Gap minus the benefit after. Stop at 20 unless the thin tail is worth extending.
  3. Broker, IRA, secondary-market TIPS. Filter by maturity year. Take the higher real yield if two CUSIPs mature the same year, unless the quote is thin.
  4. Size the face. A tight ladder sets each year so coupons still outstanding plus the maturing principal equal that year's gap. Original face is in $1,000 increments at most brokers. Confirm the ticket. You pay price times adjusted principal, plus accrued interest. The accrued interest comes back in the first coupon. It is not a fee.
  5. Hold to maturity. Coupons land in the IRA money market twice a year. Leave them as the buffer. Do not sweep them into a stock fund and forget they were the floor.
  6. Withdrawal is a tax decision. Cash at maturity sits in the IRA. In a low-income year before Social Security and IRMAA, you may withdraw it as ordinary income, or live off taxable cash and convert a planned amount instead. The ladder funds the spending. It does not pick the bracket.

Simulations

Four planning runs on the November 3, 2026 real curve: 5-year 2.35%, 7-year 2.49%, 10-year 2.65%, 20-year 2.94%, 30-year 3.09%. Each year's spending is discounted at the yield for that horizon. That present value is what a fairly priced ladder costs to deliver the spending (coupons plus maturing principal arranged to hit the target). A par-bond check at a flat 2.65% matched the annuity math to the dollar. These are ceilings for planning, not the number on tomorrow's ticket.

5. Three paths, one bad decade

Illustration, not a forecast and not a Monte Carlo. $1,000,000. Need $40,000 real per year for 10 years. Ladder case spends $350,000 on a 10-year bridge and leaves $650,000 in stocks, untouched. No-ladder cases withdraw $40,000 at the start of each year, then apply the return. Bonds in the 60/40 earn 0% real, which is generous to the no-ladder case.

Read it straight. In a good decade, skipping the ladder and staying in stocks wins, by about $68,000 on this setup. You paid for insurance you did not use. In a grinding down decade, the ladder and a 60/40 finish in the same neighborhood, and both beat selling stocks every year. In a year-one crash, the ladder finishes about $150,000 ahead of all-stock and about $84,000 ahead of 60/40, because you did not sell the recovery. That is the job. It is not free upside.

What not to do

  • Do not build this at TreasuryDirect and call it an IRA.
  • Do not buy a TIPS fund and call it a ladder.
  • Do not make every rung the same size if Social Security will cover part of later spending.
  • Do not ladder money you might need in a lump.
  • Do not sell a rung because the statement value dropped.
  • Do not forget RMDs. Age 73 is the current IRS starting age. A ladder that is the whole traditional IRA gets dismantled by that rule.

Build order

  1. Essential spending in today's dollars, minus Social Security after it starts.
  2. 20 years. Fat, then thin. Stop unless the thin tail is worth extending near a 3% real yield.
  3. Traditional IRA at the broker. Secondary-market TIPS. Sort by real yield, not coupon.
  4. Coupons plus maturing principal equal that year's gap. Hold to maturity.
  5. Leave the rest of the IRA in the guardrail portfolio.
