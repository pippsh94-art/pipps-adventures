Medicare Isn't Only a This-Year Decision

Inspired by a Geoff Schmidt / Holy Schmidt newsletter. Rewritten from official Medicare.gov rules — not a reprint, not advice.

Related: Medicare Advantage vs Medigap (age-65 window)

The real fork

When you first take Medicare, you pick a stack:

  • Original Medicare (A + B), usually with a Medigap policy and a separate Part D drug plan
  • Medicare Advantage (Part C) — a private plan that bundles A and B, usually drugs, sometimes extras

Both can be legitimate. The part people undersell: the right to choose today is not the same as the right to change your mind later. You can often leave Advantage and go back to Original Medicare during an enrollment window. Getting the Medigap policy you want after that is a different, harder question.

1. The best Medigap shot is at the beginning

Once you are 65+ and enrolled in Part B, federal law gives you a one-time 6-month Medigap Open Enrollment Period. It starts the first month you have Part B and are 65 or older. During that window you can buy any Medigap policy sold in your state. The insurer can’t use medical underwriting to reject you or price you for pre-existing conditions.

That window does not repeat every fall like Medicare Open Enrollment. After it ends, companies generally do not have to sell you a policy unless you qualify for a guaranteed-issue right. Some states add extra protections — check your state insurance department, not a Facebook ad.

Blunt take: the Medicare choice at 65 can lock or unlock options at 75.

2. There is a 12-month trial right — then it gets messy

Federal rules give two important “try it” protections:

  1. If you join Advantage when you are first eligible for Medicare at 65 and go back to Original Medicare within the first year, you get special rights to buy certain Medigap policies in your state.
  2. If you already had Medigap, drop it to try Advantage for the first time, you get a single 12-month trial to get that same Medigap policy back — if that company still sells it. If they don’t, you can buy certain other letters depending on state law and when you first became eligible for Medicare.

You generally need Original Medicare (A + B) to buy Medigap. You can’t stack Medigap on top of Advantage to pay Advantage copays.

After those trial rights expire, switching is no longer a federal guarantee. You may still drop Advantage for Original Medicare. The Medigap underwriting is the part that can say no.

Official: How Medigap works (trial rights)

3. Don’t compare them on premium alone

Advantage can look cheap. Some plans charge $0 extra premium on top of the Part B premium you still pay. That is not the same as cheap healthcare.

  • Advantage: deductibles, copays, and coinsurance when you actually use care. Networks and prior authorization are part of the deal.
  • Original + Medigap: you pay an extra premium. Depending on the letter (Plan G vs Plan N, etc.), the policy can cover most of what Original Medicare leaves behind. Original Medicare itself has no annual out-of-pocket cap unless you have supplemental coverage.

Plan N still leaves you small office/ER copays (up to $20 office / $50 ER if you aren’t admitted). Plan G is the usual “pay the Part B deductible, then the rest of Medicare-approved amounts are covered” pick for people new to Medicare on or after 2020 (Plans C and F are closed to that group).

The question is not only “what does this cost this month?” It’s “what does this cost when I actually need care?”

4. Provider access is not a rounding error

Advantage plans generally use networks. Out-of-network care may cost more or not be covered except in limited cases (emergency, urgent, out-of-area dialysis, depending on plan type).

Original Medicare lets you see any doctor or hospital that takes Medicare, anywhere in the U.S. Medigap then helps with the leftover bills. That matters if you:

  • Want a specific specialist or cancer center after a diagnosis
  • Travel a lot or spend part of the year somewhere else
  • Move during retirement (California → Idaho is a real example)

Most people shop premium. Shop access too — especially with an 18ft RV.

5. Prior authorization is part of Advantage

Many Advantage plans require the plan to approve certain services before they pay. Original Medicare uses prior authorization on a much smaller set of items.

KFF’s 2024 CMS-data analysis:

  • About 52.8 million Advantage prior-auth determinations
  • More than 90% (48.7 million) fully approved
  • 4.1 million (7.7%) denied in full or in part
  • Only 11.5% of those denials were appealed
  • Of appeals, 80.7% were partially or fully overturned

That’s not “Advantage always denies care.” It is “a non-trivial number of denials get reversed when someone fights them — and most people don’t appeal.” Delays still happen even when the answer eventually is yes. That gets more annoying as care gets complicated.

Official: KFF, 2024 prior authorization · Medicare.gov notes Advantage typically needs prior auth for certain services

6. The plan you pick can change next January

Advantage is not a one-and-done product. Premiums, deductibles, copays, drug lists, networks, and service area can change every year. Doctors can leave the network. Your health can change too.

If you’re in a Medicare plan, it sends an Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) each fall. That notice is the homework: decide whether next year’s version still fits. Don’t assume last year’s $0 plan is still the same deal.

The risk that shows up years later

At 65 you may be healthy. A local Advantage plan with a low extra premium can work extremely well.

Then you’re 75. Health changed. The specialist you want isn’t in network. You moved. You want Original Medicare plus Medigap.

Leaving Advantage for Original Medicare during an applicable enrollment period is often doable. Buying the Medigap policy you want, outside Open Enrollment or another guaranteed-issue right, can depend on your health, the insurer, and your state’s extra rules. The reason you want Medigap later (worse health) is often the same reason underwriting says no.

Medicare.gov is explicit: after the 6-month window, you may not be able to buy a policy, or it may cost more. Outside that window there is no federal guarantee an insurer will sell you one.

Official: When can I buy Medigap?

Some states add extra protection — don’t assume yours does

Federal law is the floor. A few states add stronger Medigap guaranteed-issue or birthday-rule switching. Those rules change, and they are state-specific. Confirm with your state insurance department and SHIP before betting a later switch.

If you’re moving (Bay Area → Star/Boise), check both states. Medigap is generally issued in the state where you live. Advantage networks are local. Original Medicare travels; Advantage often doesn’t travel well outside its service area.

Official: Medicare.gov — check your State Insurance Department for extra rights

Does that make Advantage a bad product?

No.

Advantage can be a good fit if you like a lower extra premium, you’re fine with that plan’s network and cost-sharing, you’ll read the ANOC every fall, and you want extras Original Medicare usually skips (some dental, vision, hearing).

Original + Medigap is a good fit if you want nationwide doctors, RV / two-state living, and a more premium-heavy, usage-light cost structure.

The job is not to pick a tribe. It’s to know what you’re buying and what it costs you if you want out later.

Default if this household uses Medicare

For nationwide doctors, an 18ft RV, and a possible California → Idaho move: Original Medicare A + B, Medigap Plan G, standalone Part D. Plan N if you want a cheaper premium and will take the office/ER copays. Advantage only if you accept a local network and yearly plan churn.

Still on a large employer plan: delaying Part B is often available. If HSA contributions still matter, delay Medicare entirely — Part A enrollment ends HSA eligibility.

Bottom line

Don’t pick the stack with the lowest premium at 65 and call it done.

  • Health can change
  • Access, cost-sharing, and prior auth matter as much as premium
  • The Medigap open-enrollment window is one-time
  • The 12-month trial rights are real — and they expire

The best option is the one that fits care you need now and leaves a level of flexibility you can live with later.
