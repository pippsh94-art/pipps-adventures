George S. Patton: Quotes That Hold Up

George Smith Patton Jr. (1885–1945)

Lines below come from his letters, diary, War As I Knew It (1947), combat notes, and the Third Army speeches — not from LinkedIn posters. Where the internet glued his name on something he didn't write, that's called out.

The Poster You Sent

Did Patton write "7 Aggressive Commandments"?

No. There is no letter, speech, order, or chapter by that title. The seven headings and the modern slogans under them ("momentum dies the moment fear gets time to organize," "the side that controls the tempo controls the outcome") are internet copy — LinkedIn/Instagram language, not 1944 Army English.

The ideas are closer to him than the wording. Speed over polish, lead by example, keep moving, hit what hurts, don't sit on a perfect plan — that is Patton. Each commandment below is mapped to the closest line that actually exists.

1. Attack Before Doubt Spreads

Poster: "Momentum dies the moment fear gets time to organize."

Not his words. Closest real line: keep moving so the other guy never gets set.

"My men don't dig foxholes. I don't want them to. Foxholes only slow up an offensive. Keep moving. And don't give the enemy time to dig one either."
— Speech to the Third Army, June 1944 (Charles M. Province transcription in The Unknown Patton)

2. Speed Beats Perfection

Poster: "A hard move now crushes a flawless move made too late."

This one is real — just said better.

"A good plan violently executed now is far better than a perfect plan executed next week."
— War As I Knew It (1947); Oxford Essential Quotations
"A good solution applied with vigor now is better than a perfect solution applied ten minutes later."
— Same idea, tighter cut; quoted in Province, The Unknown Patton (1983)

3. Lead From the Front

Poster: "People obey harder when they see you risk what you demand."

Not that sentence. He did preach example, in a letter to his son on D-Day:

"The troops I have commanded have always been well dressed, been smart saluters, been prompt and bold in action because I have personally set the example in these qualities. The influence one man can have on thousands is a never-ending source of wonder to me. You are always on parade."
— Letter to Cadet George S. Patton IV, 6 June 1944
"Wars may be fought with weapons, but they are won by men. It is the spirit of the men who follow and of the man who leads that gains the victory."
— Cavalry Journal, September 1933

4. Hit Weakness Without Mercy

Poster: "Once a crack appears exploit it before resistance regroups."

Not his wording. Same doctrine, from the D-Day letter:

"War is simple. Decide what will hurt the enemy most within the limits of your capabilities to harm him and then do it."
— Letter to his son, 6 June 1944

5. Crush Indecision Fast

Poster: "Confusion rots command so decide and make others move."

Not his words. He did tell his son not to confuse guts with gambling:

"TAKE CALCULATED RISKS. That is quite different from being rash."
— Letter to his son, 6 June 1944 (caps in the original)
"I don't want to get any messages saying, 'I am holding my position.' We are not holding a Goddamned thing. Let the Germans do that."
— Speech to the Third Army, June 1944

6. Discipline Fuels Aggression

Poster: "Force without order becomes noise and wastes power."

Not his sentence. He tied chicken-shit drill to alertness, and sloppy officers to dead soldiers:

"All through your Army careers, you men have bitched about what you call 'chicken shit drilling'. That, like everything else in this Army, has a definite purpose. That purpose is alertness."
— Speech to the Third Army, June 1944
"Officers who through laziness or a foolish desire to be popular fail to enforce discipline and the proper wearing of uniforms and equipment not in the presence of the enemy will also fail in battle, and if they fail in battle they are potential murderers."
— Letter to his son, 6 June 1944

7. Own the Initiative

Poster: "The side that controls the tempo controls the outcome."

MBA wording, not Patton. This is the real version:

"Some goddamn fool once said that flanks have got to be secure. Since then sonofabitches all over the globe have been guarding their flanks. I don't agree with that. My flanks are something for the enemy to worry about, not me. Before he finds out where my flanks are, I'll be cutting the bastard's throat."
— Conference with officers, 1 August 1944 (Hirshson, General Patton: A Soldier's Life)

More Lines That Hold Up

"Never tell people how to do things. Tell them what to do, and they will surprise you with their ingenuity."
— War As I Knew It (1947), "Reflections and Suggestions"
"There is a great deal of talk about loyalty from the bottom to the top. Loyalty from the top down is even more necessary and much less prevalent."
— War As I Knew It (1947)
"A pint of sweat will save a gallon of blood."
— Letter, 3 March 1944; also in War As I Knew It. Third Army speech used "an ounce of sweat will save a gallon of blood." Older cousins of the line exist (Rommel, even Civil War drill talk) — Patton used it, he didn't invent it.
"Every man is scared in his first battle. If he says he's not, he's a liar. … The real hero is the man who fights even though he is scared."
— Speech to the Third Army, June 1944
"All men are timid on entering any fight; whether it is the first fight or the last fight all of us are timid. Cowards are those who let their timidity get the better of their manhood."
— Letter to his son, 6 June 1944
"I find that moral courage is the most valuable and most usually absent characteristic."
— Letter to Beatrice Patton, 22 August 1943 (The Patton Papers 1940–1945, Blumenson)
"Fatigue makes cowards of all of us."
— War As I Knew It (1947)
"Have taken Trier with two divisions. What do you want me to do? Give it back?"
— 2 March 1945 reply after Eisenhower warned Trier would take four divisions (intro to War As I Knew It)
"It is foolish and wrong to mourn the men who died. Rather we should thank God that such men lived."
— Speech, Copley Plaza Hotel, Boston, 7 June 1945
"Son, only a pimp in a Louisiana whore-house carries pearl-handled revolvers. These are ivory."
— To a reporter who called his pistols pearl-handled (widely attested; exact sit-down varies by retelling)

Already in the Wisdom Collection — Verdict

The site already had four Patton lines. Two hold. Two don't, as written.

Keep: the violently-executed plan, and "Never tell people how to do things…"

Drop or flag: "If everyone is thinking alike, then somebody isn't thinking." Quote Investigator traced that family of sayings; the Patton glue job shows up around 1979 in Porter B. Williamson's I Remember General Patton's Principles as a "principle," not a sourced sentence from Patton.

Shaky: "Do not try to make circumstances fit your plans. Make plans that fit the circumstances." Quote farms repeat it. No letter, speech, or War As I Knew It citation turned up. Treat as folklore until a primary source does.

Famous Lines That Are Not (Quite) Patton

Skip these when they show up with his name attached — or keep the caveat:

"7 Aggressive Commandments…" — modern poster. Not a Patton document.
"No bastard ever won a war by dying for his country. He won it by making the other poor dumb bastard die for his." — that's George C. Scott in the 1970 film Patton. Quote Investigator found Lt. Gen. James M. Gavin (1958) recalling a coarser Africa-1943 version: "No dumb bastard ever won a war by going out and dying for his country…" So he likely said something in that family. The movie line is the one everyone quotes, not a stenographer's transcript.
"Rommel, you magnificent bastard! I read your book!" — movie only.
"Lead me, follow me, or get out of my way." — quote-book latecomer (e.g. Pocket Patriot, 2005). No wartime source.
"If everyone is thinking alike, then somebody isn't thinking." — not a sourced Patton sentence (see above).
"Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death I shall fear no evil, because I am the meanest son-of-a-bitch in the valley." — 1970s poster riff on Psalm 23. Wikiquote: no historical source that Patton said it.
The weather prayer ("Almighty and most merciful Father… restrain these immoderate rains…") — Patton ordered 250,000 copies printed with his signature. Chaplain James H. O'Neill wrote it.
